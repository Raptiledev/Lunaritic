02 — The problem

The south pole hides behind its own ground.

The Sun and Earth stay low. A ridge a few kilometres away can decide whether a lander has power, and whether it can talk to Earth. Checking one site and date today means juggling several expert tools.

  1. 01

    Ground

    How steep and rough it is, and whether that spot was really measured.

  2. 02

    Sun

    How long it clears the surrounding terrain, and the longest darkness a battery must survive.

  3. 03

    Earth

    How long Earth is in view for direct radio contact.

04 — When plans change

The launch slips. Does the site still work?

  1. 01

    Same site, new date

    The Slip Planner tests 120 landing dates against your must-haves and shows which still pass.

  2. 02

    Same date, new spot

    It searches every 100 m square within 3 km for the nearest point that passes.

  3. 03

    Side by side

    Compare up to six points. Each must pass every must-have, then a score you can read ranks them: weight × factor.

Example must-haves: 70% of the window in sunlight, at most 48 h of darkness, 20% with Earth in view, 80% favorable ground within 100 m. These are the team’s examples, not NASA requirements.

06 — The check

The sky is computed, then compared.

Live geometry

JPL NAIF SPICE

NASA’s own toolkit gives the Sun and Earth directions from the landing point, in the same reference frame as the terrain. Earth’s libration is included.

Independent check

JPL Horizons

108 saved NASA answers for Site01 and Apollo 11. The Validation page recomputes them live: agreement to about 0.00001°.

Honest limits

What we do not claim

Terrain is checked inside the 16 km area, so sunlight and Earth figures are best-case. Sunlight is not power, and Earth in view is not a radio link.
